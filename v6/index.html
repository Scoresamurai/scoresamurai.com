The Dojo

A Sensei, a Roadmap, and a belt for every set of moves you close.

Sensei Jay saw the SAT for what it is. It is a knowledge test, but also a game of moves. The same patterns come back every year, the way a martial art repeats its forms.

So he built Score Samurai to turn students into SAT Samurai Warriors. He became the Sensei and opened the Dojo. He hands out belts as each student closes the moves that used to cost them points. A belt is never taken away.

The method

The Digital SAT is a pattern recognition game, not purely an intelligence test.

Built from 1,168 real SAT questions, catalogued one at a time.

The Dojo Roadmap is the plan. It is built from the student's own misses, not from a syllabus. It lists the moves to close in the order they cost points at that score, and it is rewritten as belts are earned. No two Roadmaps are the same.

You already know the material. You are losing points to habits that have names.

The same moves, every test. The wrong answers are built from the same handful of habits, year after year. A procedure stopped one step early. A first meaning trusted too fast. A true sentence that answers a question nobody asked. Once a student can name the move, it stops working.

The Desmos work. Desmos is built into the Math section. Half of the timing problem is knowing which questions to send to it. The rest is knowing which to work by hand. We decide that question by question, until the choice is a reflex.

Weekly tracking. Speed and accuracy are tracked every week, by section, and reported to the parent in the written note. Nobody has to ask how it is going.

Why students get stuck. No two students are stuck for the same reason. One loses points to the clock. Another picks the answer that is true and still wrong. A third knows the math cold and freezes when a question looks unfamiliar.

The way I see it, AI can answer the question. It cannot see why this student keeps missing it. That is the difference between an app and a Sensei.

The fourth practice test came back within twenty points of the first three. The family had bought more tests and the student had taken them. The score had not moved, because nothing about how the questions were being read had changed.

The Diagnostic put the fourteen misses side by side. Nine of them were two patterns: answer choices that were true but did not answer the question asked, and punctuation chosen by ear. Both have a name and a rule.

The sessions closed those two first, and the reading misses on the midpoint test were four.

The gap to 1350 was never fourteen questions. It was two habits.

1090

An A in Algebra 2 and a score that said otherwise, so the student had decided the problem was nerves and the parents had half believed it. The misses said something more useful.

On every Math module there was a question that asked for 2x plus 3 and got x, worked correctly and answered wrong. On every module the last four questions were blank, because the first eighteen had taken too long.

Two habits, one of them fixable on the spot with Desmos doing the arithmetic.

The score moved a full band, and "bad at tests" had never been true.

Questions

Frequently asked.

Anything not here, ask on the Diagnostic form. We answer every one.

How long does SAT prep take?

It depends on the size of the gap and the runway before the test date. That is what the Diagnostic measures. Starting late turns the work into a sprint. Starting far too early turns it into drift.

The honest starting point is where the Diagnostic says the student is, not where the calendar says they should be.

What is a good SAT score?

Good is whatever gets you into the schools on your list. For one student that's a 1200, and for another it's a 1450. The Diagnostic starts with your list and works back to the score each school expects, then to the moves that get you there.

Do colleges require the SAT?

More of the hardest schools to get into require it every year.

Required now (as of September 2026): Brown, Caltech, Cornell, Dartmouth, Harvard, Johns Hopkins, MIT, Penn, Stanford and Yale.

Required from the 2027-28 cycle (as of September 2026): Columbia and Princeton. Both stay test-optional for students applying this fall.

Many other schools stay test-optional. A strong score still helps there, and policies change, so check each school's own page.
