The method

Why it works.

The method hundreds of students have already put their trust in.

Sensei Jay has spent nearly a decade teaching students across the country to beat this test, and those years showed him one thing: most students already know the material. The points go to traps nobody ever taught them to recognize.

No tutor can honestly promise a score. What we promise is the work: every session, every week, without exception. Speed and accuracy are tracked weekly and reported to parents, so a family sees the program working without having to ask, and the student always knows what comes next.

Why students get stuck

No two students are stuck for the same reason. One loses points to the clock, another guesses on the reading and calls it a hunch, a third knows the math cold and freezes when a question looks unfamiliar. On the questions that decide a score, the point goes to the clock, to a wrong answer built from half-finished work, or to a choice that is perfectly true and answers a question nobody asked. Every one of those is a failure to recognize what the question is doing. Recognition is what we train.

Some students arrive needing 300 points and some arrive chasing the last 100, which are the hardest on the test. The method holds at both ends because the traps are the same at every score. Only the surface changes.

Why not an app

An app can answer the question. It cannot tell you why you keep missing it.

Most students who come to us have already tried the apps, the videos and the free AI tools, and the information was never the problem. A tool will never notice someone going through the motions. Sensei Jay teaches every session personally and keeps the roster small enough to know each student's name and their particular traps.

That is what a family is paying for: a person who tracks how a student thinks, builds the next session around it, and makes sure the work gets done.

What a trap looks like

A choice can be true and still wrong.

Every wrong answer on this test was built from something a real student does when the clock is running: a correct procedure stopped one step early, a first meaning trusted too fast, a true sentence that answers a question nobody asked. Every trap in the Pattern Codex has a shape like these, a name, and a rule that closes it. The Diagnostic tells a student which of them are already costing points.

Questions

Frequently asked.

Anything not here, ask in the Dojo or on the call.

How long does SAT prep take?

It depends on the size of the gap and the runway left before the test date, which is what the diagnostic is for. Most students run two 50-minute sessions a week and sit the test once the plan has closed the gap the diagnostic found. Starting late turns the work into a sprint, and starting far too early tends to turn it into drift. The next test date is always closer than it feels, so the honest starting point is where the diagnostic says the student is, rather than where the calendar says they should be.

What is a good SAT score for selective schools?

At the most selective schools, the middle of the admitted class now sits at 1500 and above, and a 1450 keeps a student in the conversation. One tier down, a score between 1350 and 1450 does the work. The number that matters is where the admitted range starts at the school the student wants, and that is the number the Dojo Roadmap is built to reach.

Do colleges require the SAT in 2026?

Some do. Harvard, Stanford, Georgetown and the University of Miami all require an SAT or ACT score from first-year applicants right now, and a school that requires the test reads it as part of the academic record rather than as a bonus. Most of the schools families ask about are still test optional, including Duke, Michigan, USC, Boston College, Wisconsin, Penn State, Oregon and Rutgers, which means a student chooses whether the score is considered. Two more have already set a date to bring the requirement back: Princeton and Notre Dame both return to requiring scores for students applying in the 2027 to 2028 cycle, the students who would enter college in fall 2028. At the other end, the University of California campuses including UCLA do not look at scores at all, and a score cannot help or hurt there. Policies have been moving back toward requiring the test more often than away from it, and every school sets its own, so the school's own admissions page is the only source worth trusting on the day you apply. All of this is current as of September 2026. A student aiming high should plan on the score counting.

How is this different from an app?

An app can grade an answer, but it can't see why a student keeps missing the same kind of question, and it will never notice someone going through the motions on practice. We start from the specific traps costing a student points, build each session around them, and track speed and accuracy from week to week, with Sensei Jay there every week to see that the work gets done. Students have free access to more SAT material than they could ever use, which is a bit like having the run of a well-equipped gym with nobody to build the program, run the sessions, or check that the work got done. A trainer is what was missing, and that's the role we fill.
